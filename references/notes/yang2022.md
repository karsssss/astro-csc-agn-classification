# Yang et al. 2022 — MUWCLASS: Classifying Unidentified X-Ray Sources in the CSC Using a Multiwavelength ML Approach

**Citation:** Yang, H., Hare, J., Kargaltsev, O., Volkov, I., Chen, S., & Rangelov, B. 2022, ApJ, 941, 104. https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ac952b
**File:** `references/yang2022_csc_muwclass_supervised.pdf` (35 pp.)
**One-line summary:** Supervised Random Forest pipeline (MUWCLASS) that classifies Chandra Source Catalog v2 (CSCv2) X-ray sources into 8 classes using X-ray + multiwavelength (Gaia/2MASS/WISE) features, with Monte Carlo sampling over measurement uncertainty, SMOTE oversampling, AGN-specific reddening correction, and a novel per-source confidence threshold (CT).

## 1. Problem setup and classes

- Training data (TD): 2941 literature-verified CSCv2 sources across 8 classes (Table 1): AGN (1390), YSO (1004), LM-STAR (207), HM-STAR (118), NS (87), LMXB (65), CV (44), HMXB (26). Heavily imbalanced — AGN and YSO dominate.
- Applied to 66,369 "good CSC sample" (GCS) sources (S/N ≥ 3, PU < 1″, no conf/extent flags) — 21% of the full CSCv2.
- Directly analogous to our problem (AGN vs. non-AGN/galaxy from CSC X-ray + multiwavelength), but theirs is 8-way rather than our AGN/non-AGN/silver 3-class scheme.

## 2. Features used (X-ray + MW) — our main point of comparison

Full feature list before selection: 54 features (4 X-ray fluxes, 3 X-ray HRs, 2 X-ray variability features, 3 optical mags, 3 NIR mags, 3 IR mags, 36 colors). Final selected set: **29 features** (Table 2), after an importance-based cut (see §4 below).

- **X-ray (per-observation, weighted-average across multiple detections):**
  - `F_s, F_m, F_h` — soft (0.5–1.2 keV) / medium (1.2–2 keV) / hard (2–7 keV) band fluxes; `F_b` broadband (0.5–7 keV) computed by summing the three (Eq. 2a/2b for mean/variance)
  - Fluxes modeled with a **Fechner (split-normal) distribution** using CSCv2's mode + asymmetric 1σ lo/hi limits (Eq. 1a/1b) — richer uncertainty model than a plain Gaussian
  - `HR_ms, HR_hm, HR_h(ms)` — hardness ratios (medium-soft, hard-medium, combined hard-vs-(medium+soft))
  - `P_inter` — inter-observation variability probability, from a chi-square test on the broadband flux across observations (Eq. 4a/4b)
  - `P_intra` — intra-observation variability, the max Kuiper's-test probability across all observations
  - Off-axis angle > 10′ detections dropped (worse PSF/confusion); pileup-flagged detections dropped
- **Multiwavelength (crossmatched within combined 2σ PU radius, nearest match only):**
  - Gaia EDR3: `G, BP, RP` magnitudes
  - 2MASS: `J, H, K`
  - WISE (via AllWISE/CatWISE2020/unWISE, W1/W2 preferring CatWISE2020, W3 only from AllWISE): `W1, W2` retained as magnitudes; **W3 magnitude itself dropped after feature selection, but W1−W3 and W2−W3 colors kept** (colors more informative than the raw magnitude — relevant since our pipeline also builds `WISE_W1_W2`/`WISE_W2_W3` colors)
  - All pairwise **colors** among {G, BP, RP, J, H, K, W1, W2} (W3 excluded except via the two colors above) — 36 colors total before selection
  - No W4 band (shallow depth + large PU → more confusion)
- Everything is **standardized** (z-scored, Eq. 6) across the full TD before modeling; fluxes and optical/NIR/IR fluxes (converted from mag via zero-point/effective-bandwidth, Table 3) are log10'd and divided by broadband X-ray flux (except F_b itself) to form flux-ratio features and mitigate distance dependence.
- Missing MW data is common (optical missing 24%, NIR 38%, IR 23%; 8% missing all three) — X-ray features are essentially always present (≥1 band for 100%, 3 bands for 94%).

## 3. Feature selection method

Trained RF with all 54 features, ran **LOOCV × 1000 MC samples**, computed Gini-impurity-based feature importance (mean ± std across runs, Figure 4). Added a **random noise feature** (importance 0.40% ± 0.05%) as a null baseline and kept only features with importance > 1% (2.5× the random feature) → 29 features. Most important single feature: `HR_ms` (~8%); `H`, `K`, `P_intra` also high. `max_features` for RF = 6 = √29.

## 4. Random Forest configuration

- Algorithm: scikit-learn `RandomForestClassifier` (Breiman 2001; Pedregosa et al. 2011), Gini impurity splitting criterion.
- **Default hyperparameters used and found near-optimal via LOOCV sweep:**
  - `n_estimators = 100` (performance flat within ~0.5% for n_estimators ≥ 50; runtime scales linearly with it)
  - `max_depth = None` (grow until pure or < 2 samples per leaf)
  - `max_features = sqrt(n_features)` = 6 (for their 29 features)
  - Performance is largely insensitive to hyperparameters near their defaults — they explicitly did **not** find much gain from tuning.
- **Algorithm comparison (Table 4 / Figure 5, macro-avg ROC-AUC across all 8 classes):**

  | Algorithm | Accuracy | Balanced Acc. | Macro F1 | MCC | LOOCV runtime (s) | AUC |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Random Forest | 0.886 | 0.702 | 0.681 | 0.829 | 967 | 0.97 |
  | Extra Trees | 0.882 | 0.680 | 0.664 | 0.822 | 770 | 0.97 |
  | Gradient Boosting | 0.874 | 0.720 | 0.665 | 0.815 | **5080** | 0.97 |
  | Bagging | 0.867 | 0.668 | 0.628 | 0.803 | 841 | 0.91 |
  | SVC | 0.856 | 0.651 | 0.619 | 0.787 | 6240 | 0.90 |
  | KNN (k=5) | 0.835 | 0.651 | 0.593 | 0.760 | 733 | 0.85 |
  | Decision Tree | 0.882 | 0.680 | 0.592 | 0.766 | 752 | 0.80 |

  RF, Extra Trees, and Gradient Boosting are essentially tied on AUC/accuracy; **GB is ~5–7× slower** than RF/Extra Trees in their setup. **They chose RF mainly for interpretability/community familiarity and speed, not because it strictly outperformed boosting** — GB (gradient boosting) actually has the best balanced accuracy of the group (0.720 vs. RF's 0.702).
- They explicitly designed MUWCLASS to be **algorithm-agnostic** — "any user can easily replace the RF algorithm with some other algorithm" — since the data is already standardized and imputed before fitting.
- 1000× Monte Carlo (MC) resampling of feature values from their assumed per-feature error distributions is done for every classified source (and every TD source during LOOCV) to propagate measurement uncertainty into the classification probabilities; this is layered *on top of* whatever classifier is used, not a property of RF specifically.

## 5. Class imbalance handling (SMOTE)

- Applied SMOTE (Chawla et al. 2011, `imbalanced-learn`) to oversample all minority classes **up to the size of the most populous class (AGN, 1390)**, i.e., roughly ~11× oversampling for the smallest class (HMXB, 26 → 1390).
- Standard settings: synthetic points generated between a real minority-class point and one of its k=4 nearest same-class neighbors (default SMOTE parameter, tunable).
- Order of operations in the pipeline (Figure 2): reddening/absorption applied to AGNs → standardize → **SMOTE** → replace missing values with flag (−100) → train RF.
  - i.e., SMOTE runs on **already-standardized** features, and missing-value flagging happens **after** SMOTE (so synthetic sources are generated only from the subset of non-missing dimensions actually present — they don't say explicitly how missing values are handled inside SMOTE itself, but the ordering implies SMOTE operates on real-valued/standardized data before the −100 flag exists).
- Caveats they raise: SMOTE can create "artificial linear structures" in feature space (Figure 3) and is fragile if a minority-class outlier with a wrong/noisy label gets used as a SMOTE parent — "it is important to have a clean TD." No comparison against class-weighting (`class_weight` in sklearn) or other imbalance techniques was actually run; they just note weighting as an alternative.

## 6. Extinction/absorption corrections applied to AGNs

Rationale: >99% of TD AGNs come from high-Galactic-latitude surveys (|b| > 10°), so they're under-reddened/under-absorbed relative to an AGN that would be seen through the Galactic plane. Left uncorrected, this biases classification against real AGNs found near the plane. Correction is applied **only to AGNs in the training data**, direction-specific to wherever the *target* (unclassified) source being classified is located — not a fixed global correction.

- **X-ray absorption:** TD AGN X-ray fluxes are multiplied by an absorption correction factor
  `C_abs = ∫ f(E) exp(−N_H σ(E)) dE / ∫ f(E) dE` (Eq. 5), assuming an intrinsic power-law spectrum (Γ=2), integrated over the CSCv2 band boundaries, using Wilms et al. (2000) photoelectric absorption cross sections σ(E).
  - `N_H` (hydrogen column density) is derived from optical extinction: `N_H (cm⁻²) = (2.21 ± 0.09) × 10²¹ × A_V` (Güver & Özel 2009), with `A_V = 3.1 × E(B−V)` (Fitzpatrick 1999) from the **SFD dust map** (Schlegel et al. 1998; Schlafly & Finkbeiner 2011, 6.1′ resolution).
  - They deliberately use the SFD *reddening* map rather than direct HI column maps because HI alone underestimates absorption (doesn't capture molecular H₂) and has coarser resolution.
- **Optical/NIR/IR magnitudes:** reddened using the same E(B−V) at the effective wavelength of each band (Table 3 gives λ_eff, W_eff per band), via the `extinction` Python package. Reddened magnitudes that would fall fainter than a survey's limiting magnitude are dropped (treated as non-detections) rather than kept as unrealistically faint values.
- **Computational shortcut:** sky is binned into ~2000 bins of constant E(B−V) (bin size 25 mmag, similar to the SFD map's own reddening uncertainty) to avoid recomputing per-source; ~30s per classification-bin on one CPU core.
- Known limitation they flag: SFD map is less reliable at low Galactic latitude (|b| < 5°), and the correction can't capture extreme local reddening gradients; this is exactly the situation for point-source Galactic-plane classification.

**Relevance to our pipeline:** our AGN sample explicitly needs this if we're mixing high-latitude "gold" AGN training labels with sources anywhere in `ra`/`dec`/`gal_l`/`gal_b` — worth checking whether `gal_b` in our catalog correlates with `agn_positive` rate the way it does in this paper (they find AGNs in CCGCS lack any Galactic-plane concentration, Figure 11, precisely because the reddening bias suppresses detections there in the first place).

## 7. Confidence Threshold (CT) definition

CT quantifies, per source, how confidently the top-probability class beats the runner-up, accounting for the MC-derived uncertainty on each class probability:

```
CT = min over class≠predicted_class of:  (P_predicted_class − P_class) / (ΔP_predicted_class + ΔP_class)
```

(Eq. 7) — i.e., the *smallest* number of combined-uncertainty units by which the predicted class beats any other single class. `ΔP` (the std dev of the class probability across the 1000 MC samples) is floored at 1e-5 to avoid divide-by-zero when a class has zero variance.

- They chose **CT ≥ 2** as their working confidence cut, based on Figure 6 (metrics vs. CT sweep): at CT=2, accuracy = 97.0%, balanced accuracy = 79.0%, F1 = 79.5%, MCC = 95.0%, completeness = 81.0%, balanced completeness = 56.9% — a tradeoff point where completeness starts dropping faster than accuracy improves.
- CT is fundamentally about **separation between the top two classes relative to their combined uncertainty**, not an absolute probability cutoff (e.g., "P > 0.9") — this only works because they already have a per-source, per-class probability *distribution* from MC sampling, not a single point estimate. Adopting CT as-is in a LightGBM/CatBoost pipeline would require an equivalent source of per-class probability uncertainty (e.g., MC-Dropout-style resampling of input features, or bootstrap/ensemble variance across multiple trained models), since gradient-boosted trees don't give this for free the way repeated MC-sampled RF fits do.

## 8. Per-class precision/recall (Figure 8, Table 5)

All-classifications recall / precision (%), vs. confident (CT ≥ 2) recall / precision:

| Class | Recall (all) | Recall (CT≥2) | Precision (all) | Precision (CT≥2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AGN | 93 | 99 | 98 | 99 |
| CV | 55 | 73 | 48 | 73 |
| HM-STAR | 72 | 79 | 67 | 86 |
| LM-STAR | 86 | 96 | 81 | 92 |
| HMXB | 69 | 75 | 75 | 80 |
| LMXB | 26 | 25 | 19 | 10 |
| NS | 68 | 86 | 51 | 80 |
| YSO | 94 | 98 | 93 | 98 |

Key takeaways:
- **AGN and YSO (the two most populous classes) perform best**, recall/precision > 98% after the CT≥2 cut — consistent with them having the most TD examples.
- **LMXB is the worst-performing class** (confident precision only 10%, recall 25%) — heavily confused with NS, AGN, and CV. Driven by (a) very few LMXB TD examples (65) and (b) ~63% of LMXBs lacking any MW counterpart (large distances + reddening), making them look like isolated NSs.
- Overall metrics: accuracy 88.6% → 97.0% after CT≥2; balanced accuracy 70.2% → 79.0%; balanced completeness drops from 100% → 56.9% after the cut (i.e., the confidence cut disproportionately removes minority-class sources).
- External validation crossmatches (SIMBAD, 4XMM-DR10, FIRST-NVSS-SDSS, WISE-SDSS) broadly corroborate the AGN/YSO recall (93.6–100%) but confirm CV/LMXB/XRB classes remain unreliable (e.g., CV recall only 25% against 4XMM-DR10).

## 9. Other points relevant to our LightGBM + CatBoost pipeline

- **Feature importance methodology** (permutation-style, mean-decrease-in-impurity with a random/noise feature as the significance baseline) is directly reusable with LightGBM/CatBoost's built-in importances or SHAP (which we already use) — the "keep features with importance > threshold-derived-from-a-random-feature" trick is a cleaner cutoff rule than an arbitrary top-K.
- **Missing-value strategy differs from ours**: they use a single large negative sentinel (−100) for all missing values (post-standardization) rather than imputation, specifically because they found mean-imputation biases sources like isolated NSs that are missing MW data *for physical reasons* (real absence of a counterpart, not just non-detection noise) — this is worth weighing against our current SimpleImputer(median) + missing-indicator-column approach. Sentinel-flagging is easy for tree-based models (LightGBM/CatBoost both handle it natively, and CatBoost/LightGBM can also take NaN directly without imputation at all, which more faithfully preserves "missingness is informative" — arguably better than either their sentinel approach or our imputation).
- **They explicitly note the pipeline is algorithm-agnostic by design** and encourage swapping in other classifiers — nothing about their preprocessing (standardization → SMOTE → missing-flag) is RF-specific, so it transfers directly to a LightGBM/CatBoost setup, **except** that gradient-boosted trees generally don't need standardization (scale-invariant splits) and can accept raw/NaN features, so some of their preprocessing steps (z-scoring, −100 sentinel) may be unnecessary overhead for us and could be simplified.
- **SMOTE + gradient boosting caution**: SMOTE's linear-interpolation artifacts (Figure 3) are a known bad interaction with boosted trees that can overfit to synthetic linear structure; consider `class_weight`/`scale_pos_weight` (LightGBM) or `class_weights`/`auto_class_weights` (CatBoost) as a first-pass imbalance strategy instead of/before SMOTE, given both libraries support this natively and it avoids the artificial-structure risk they flag.
- **Selection-bias framing (§5.2, §7.1) is directly applicable to our silver/gold scheme**: their TD AGNs are systematically brighter (higher X-ray flux, higher MW-counterpart fraction) than the general classified population, inflating apparent performance metrics relative to true population-level performance. Same risk applies to our `agn_positive`/`silver_agn` gold/silver split — worth checking whether our gold-labeled AGNs are similarly biased toward brighter/higher-S/N sources relative to the full `Multiwave_UMAP_6_26_26.csv` population before trusting LOOCV-style validation metrics at face value.
- **Reddening/absorption correction (§6 above) has no analog yet in our notebooks** (`unsupervised.ipynb` etc. don't currently apply an SFD-based correction to AGN training labels) — this is probably the single most actionable methodological gap this paper highlights for our pipeline, since we already compute `gal_l`/`gal_b` and could pull E(B−V) from an SFD/Bayestar map the same way.
